# Resources for writing and designing adventure games

A selected reading and viewing list for story, characters, puzzles, and player experience in point-and-click adventures. These resources focus on creative and design decisions rather than programming.

## Start here: adventure-game design

- **[“Why Adventure Games Suck” — Ron Gilbert](https://grumpygamer.com/why_adventure_games_suck)**  
  A short, influential critique of unfair puzzles and opaque interactions. Useful as a checklist for avoiding arbitrary solutions and making the player’s reasoning feel respected.

- **[The Art of Point-and-Click Adventure Games — Bitmap Books](https://www.bitmapbooks.com/products/the-art-of-point-and-click-adventure-games)**  
  A visual history of the genre, with discussion and examples from classic and modern adventures. Read it to see how tone, interface, art, and storytelling have changed across the genre.

- **[The Art of Game Design: A Book of Lenses — Jesse Schell](https://www.schellgames.com/art-of-game-design)**  
  A broad design handbook. Its questions and lenses are useful for checking whether a story beat, interaction, or puzzle is engaging and understandable from the player’s perspective.

## Story, characters, and dialogue

- **[The Game Narrative Toolbox — Tobias Heussner, Toiya Kristen Finley, Ann Lemay, and Jennifer Brandes Hepler](https://books.google.com/books?q=The+Game+Narrative+Toolbox+Heussner)**  
  A practical introduction to narrative design, including story structure, characters, dialogue, and how narrative fits into play. Useful for turning a premise into material that works in an interactive game.

- **[Interactive Storytelling for Video Games — Josiah Lebowitz and Chris Klug](https://books.google.com/books?q=Interactive+Storytelling+for+Video+Games+Lebowitz+Klug)**  
  Focuses on building stories around player choice and interaction. Helpful when deciding what the player controls, what choices matter, and how to keep a story coherent while allowing exploration.

- **[Writing for Video Games — Steve Ince](https://books.google.com/books?q=Writing+for+Video+Games+Steve+Ince)**  
  A writer-oriented guide to the craft and practical constraints of game writing, including dialogue and collaboration. Use it to think about writing that supports play rather than simply placing a linear script inside a game.

## Puzzle and game design

- **[Puzzlecraft: The Ultimate Guide to Making Every Kind of Puzzle — Mike Selinker and Thomas Snyder](https://books.google.com/books?q=Puzzlecraft+Mike+Selinker+Thomas+Snyder)**  
  A hands-on guide to puzzle construction and clueing. It is not limited to adventure games, but its focus on clear rules, satisfying deductions, and fair challenge transfers well to item and environmental puzzles.

- **[The Game Design Workshop — Tracy Fullerton](https://books.google.com/books?q=The+Game+Design+Workshop+Tracy+Fullerton)**  
  Covers iterative design, prototyping, and playtesting. Apply its methods to test whether players understand goals, discover useful clues, and can explain why a puzzle solution makes sense.

## Learn from finished adventures

Play or replay these as design case studies, not just as stories. Keep notes on what the player knows at each point, what they can try, how the game signals progress, and what happens when they get stuck.

- **Broken Sword: The Shadow of the Templars** — study investigation, scene-to-scene pacing, character-led exposition, and how a grounded mystery makes room for comedy.
- **Grim Fandango** — study worldbuilding, voice, character arcs, and how a strong setting can unify a large episodic journey.
- **The Secret of Monkey Island** — study comic voice, memorable characters, readable puzzle logic, and how a game teaches its rules.
- **Day of the Tentacle** — study how a clear central mechanic can generate story situations and connected puzzles.
- **Thimbleweed Park** — study how a modern game deliberately uses and comments on classic adventure conventions.

## A practical way to use the list

1. Read Gilbert’s essay, then write down the assumptions a player needs for each puzzle in your own idea.
2. Choose one narrative-design book and one puzzle-design book; use them to outline the story and build a small set of connected puzzles.
3. Analyze one reference game scene by scene. Record the player’s goal, available information, meaningful choices, and any hint or feedback after an unsuccessful attempt.
4. Test the outline with someone unfamiliar with it. Ask them to think aloud, and revise points where they cannot infer what the game expects or why a solution works.
