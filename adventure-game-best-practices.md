# Adventure game writing and design best practices

Practical principles distilled from the resources and example games in [Resources for writing and designing adventure games](adventure-game-writing-resources.md).

## Story and player experience

- **Give the player a clear, compelling goal.** Make the immediate objective understandable, even when the larger mystery unfolds gradually.
- **Make story happen through play.** Let investigation, choices, conversations, and puzzle solutions reveal character and move the plot forward instead of relying only on exposition.
- **Build a distinctive, coherent world.** Use setting, tone, and recurring details to make scenes memorable and support the story’s themes.
- **Write characters with recognizable voices and purposes.** Dialogue should reveal personality, provide useful context, or create an interesting interaction; avoid making every conversation feel interchangeable.
- **Keep the experience paced.** Alternate discovery, conversation, puzzle-solving, and story developments so that no one activity overwhelms the adventure.
- **Give choices a reason to exist.** Let players explore and express curiosity; when a choice affects the story, make its consequences understandable and meaningful.

## Fair, satisfying puzzles

- **Make solutions follow from clues and established rules.** A player should be able to explain why an answer works, rather than needing to guess what the designer had in mind.
- **Teach puzzle logic before demanding mastery.** Introduce a mechanic or rule in a low-pressure situation, then build on it.
- **Make important information discoverable.** Put clues where attentive players can find them, and reinforce critical facts when a puzzle depends on remembering them.
- **Connect actions to outcomes.** After an attempt, communicate what changed or why it failed so players can adjust their reasoning.
- **Avoid arbitrary item combinations and hidden prerequisites.** If an object or fact matters, establish its relevance through the world, dialogue, or earlier events.
- **Keep the player moving.** Avoid irreversible dead ends and ensure essential progress does not depend on one obscure optional interaction.
- **Use puzzle variety with a consistent voice.** Mix observation, deduction, conversation, and environmental interaction, while keeping challenges appropriate to the game’s tone.

## Design and iteration

- **Design around the player’s knowledge.** For each scene, note the player’s goal, what they know, what they can try, and what feedback follows.
- **Make the game’s central mechanics productive.** A strong recurring mechanic can generate connected puzzles and story situations, as in *Day of the Tentacle*.
- **Prototype puzzle sequences early.** Check that clues arrive in a useful order and that each step leads naturally to the next.
- **Playtest with people who do not know the solution.** Ask them to think aloud; observe where they get stuck or misunderstand the game, then revise the clueing or feedback.
- **Treat confusion as design feedback.** If multiple testers miss the same inference, clarify what the game communicates rather than blaming the player.
- **Study examples deliberately.** When replaying games such as *Broken Sword*, *Grim Fandango*, or *Monkey Island*, track goals, clues, pacing, character moments, and how the game handles failed attempts.

## Quick review checklist

Before considering a scene or puzzle finished, ask:

- Can the player state what they are trying to accomplish?
- Are the relevant clues and rules available before they are needed?
- Does the solution make sense in the game’s world?
- Does the game respond clearly to useful and unsuccessful attempts?
- Does this moment advance the story, develop a character, or create an enjoyable challenge?
- Can a new player make progress without guessing or getting trapped?
